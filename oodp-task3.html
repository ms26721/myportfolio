Task 3: Reflection on Skill Development

Advanced Object-Oriented Design & Programming · Capstone Project: End of Module Reflective Portfolio

3.1 Academic skills

Critical thinking and analysis: I learned to break complex problems into forces and responsibilities before writing code, then compare alternative designs against criteria such as coupling, cohesion and testability. Questioning whether a pattern was needed at all, as in the notification factory, became as important as applying one.

Ethical considerations in AI-driven OO design: I became aware that class hierarchies can propagate bias: an assumption encoded in a base class, such as a default priority for a customer category, is silently inherited by every subclass and applied at scale. AI-enabled objects also raise transparency and explainability challenges, so I gave classifiers an explain() method returning the label, confidence and key features, and used a logging decorator to record decisions for auditability. I see the OO designer as responsible for keeping AI components testable, avoiding “black box” coupling by isolating models behind interfaces, and supporting audit through clean, inspectable design.

Problem-solving: I approached coding challenges incrementally: reproduce the problem, write a failing test, implement the smallest fix and refactor. Refactoring the oversized TicketManager under the protection of tests showed me that maintainability improves through small, safe steps.

Research and self-study: I consulted Gamma et al. (1994) for pattern intent, Martin (2017) for architectural layering, OWASP guidance for secure coding and Sculley et al. (2015) on technical debt in machine-learning systems, which shaped my thinking on registries and feature stores. Python documentation and case studies helped bridge theory and practice.

3.2 Employability skills

Time management: balancing coursework with other commitments, I planned weekly goals, timeboxed exercises and split the Capstone Project into milestones (design, core domain, patterns, testing, report) tracked on a Kanban board. Starting the testing work early prevented a last-minute crunch.

Communication and literacy: discussion forums and peer reviews required me to justify design decisions clearly and concisely. UML class and sequence diagrams helped communicate structure to peers, and writing this portfolio strengthened my technical writing.

Resilience and interpersonal skills: setbacks such as the elusive race condition and brittle mocks taught me to persist methodically rather than patch symptoms. In group activities and peer code reviews I learned to give constructive feedback and accept critique of my own designs, which improved both my code and my collaboration. These skills are directly relevant to my aspirations in IT consultancy, where clear design reasoning and client communication matter as much as technical skill.
